S1. Since \(x_n \to a\), by the definition of convergence, for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(N_1\in\mathbb{N}\) such that
\[
n\ge N_1 \implies d(x_n,a)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\]

S2. Since \(x_n \to b\), by the definition of convergence, for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(N_2\in\mathbb{N}\) such that
\[
n\ge N_2 \implies d(x_n,b)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\]

S3. Let \(\varepsilon>0\) be arbitrary, and set
\[
N=\max\{N_1,N_2\}.
\]
Then for every \(n\ge N\), both
\[
d(x_n,a)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
\quad\text{and}\quad
d(x_n,b)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\]

S4. By the triangle inequality,
\[
d(a,b)\le d(a,x_n)+d(x_n,b).
\]
Since a metric is symmetric, \(d(a,x_n)=d(x_n,a)\). Therefore, for every \(n\ge N\),
\[
d(a,b)
\le d(x_n,a)+d(x_n,b)
<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
=\varepsilon.
\]

S5. Hence \(d(a,b)<\varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). If \(d(a,b)>0\), choosing \(\varepsilon=d(a,b)\) would give
\[
d(a,b)<d(a,b),
\]
which is impossible. Therefore,
\[
d(a,b)=0.
\]

S6. By definiteness of the metric, \(d(a,b)=0\) implies \(a=b\). Thus the limit of a convergent sequence in a metric space is unique.
