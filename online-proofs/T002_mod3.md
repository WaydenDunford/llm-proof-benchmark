S1. Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space, and let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $X$ that converges to both $a$ and $b$.

S2. Let $\epsilon > 0$ be given. By the definition of convergence for the limit $a$, there exists an integer $N_1$ such that for all $n \ge N_1$, $d(x_n, a) < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.

S3. Similarly, by the definition of convergence for the limit $b$, there exists an integer $N_2$ such that for all $n \ge N_2$, $d(x_n, b) < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.

S4. Choose $N = \max(N_1, N_2)$. For any $n \ge N$, the conditions $d(x_n, a) < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$ and $d(x_n, b) < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$ hold simultaneously.

S5. By the triangle inequality property of the metric $d$, the distance between $a$ and $b$ satisfies $d(a, b) \le d(a, x_n) + d(x_n, b)$.

S6. Because a metric is symmetric, $d(a, x_n) = d(x_n, a)$, which allows the inequality to be written as $d(a, b) \le d(x_n, a) + d(x_n, b)$.

S7. Substituting the bounds from step S4 into this inequality for any $n \ge N$ yields $d(a, b) < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2}$, which simplifies to $d(a, b) < \epsilon$.

S8. Since $\epsilon > 0$ was chosen arbitrarily and metric distances are non-negative, the fact that $d(a, b) < \epsilon$ for every positive $\epsilon$ necessitates that $d(a, b) = 0$.

S9. By the definiteness property of a metric space, $d(a, b) = 0$ implies that $a = b$.
